**Poetry and Structure**

In both its construction and its imagery Psalm 121 illustrates well for us the poetic beauty of the Psalms. As are virtually all the Psalms in the Songs of Ascents, this psalm is oriented to the Jerusalem temple. The superscript informs us that it is “A Song of Ascents,” a psalm sung by the faithful as they made their journey to the holy city to observe its annual feasts. They were sung by Israelites as they approached (“ascent”) the city for festivities, having the “holy hill” (Mount Zion) and surrounding hills in view. That journey, in turn, becomes a picture of the journey of life under the watchful guardianship of God (121:8).

The atmosphere is one of a potentially dangerous journey; the theme is that of confident trust. Here the theme of confidence is developed through the psalm by an overview of God’s protective care. Repeatedly he is portrayed as a very personal guardian (“my help,” “your keeper,” “your shade”). The key word of the psalm is “keep” or “keeper,” appearing six times (שׁמר), essentially synonymous with “help” (121:1, 2). Throughout the psalm it is “the LORD” who gives “help” and who “keeps” (121:2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8). He is his people’s watchful guardian, and the faithful look to him as such.

Alec Motyer notes a distinct progression in the psalm. In 121:1–2 the Lord is presented as Creator. Then in 121:3–4, with its reference to Israel, he is presented as redeemer and covenant Lord. And finally in 121:5–6 he is presented as companion, standing alongside his people to help. “This threefold theology of Creator, Redeemer and Companion, then leads into the climax (vv. 7–8), keeping from all evil (7a), the whole person (7b), in every activity (8a), and for all time, starting now (8b).”

The poem is symmetrically constructed in four stanzas with two verses each. The successive stanzas “build on each other, particularly through the repetition of the word _watch_” (or “keep/keeper”). Each verse is constructed with two parallel lines in which the second line supplements or completes the thought of the first or heightens it in some way. The two verses of each stanza also parallel one another.

121:2 completes the thought of 121:1, linking them by the catchword “help”; 121:4 extends the thought of 121:3, using the catchword “keep”; 121:6 completes the thought of 121:5 by the association of sun and shade; and 121:8 heightens the thought of 121:7, again linking them by “keep.” Let’s look at this more carefully.

In verse 121:1a the psalmist looks to the hills; in verse 121:1b we find that he is looking to the hills for help. In verse 121:2a he affirms that his help comes from the LORD, Israel’s covenant God; in verse 121:2b he extends the thought by declaring that Israel’s God is the Creator of all things. In both verses the second line parallels the first in some kind of completive sense. This is stanza 1. But notice further not just the second lines but that 121:2 itself parallels 121:1, completing its thought:

1 I lift up my eyes to the hills.  
 From where does my help come?  
2 My help comes from the LORD,  
 who made heaven and earth.

So also in stanza 2, the second verse parallels the first by extending its thought:

3 He will not let your foot be moved;  
 he who keeps you will not slumber.  
4 Behold, he who keeps Israel  
 will neither slumber nor sleep.

So also in stanza 3, the second verse parallels the first by completing its thought:

5 The LORD is your keeper;  
 the LORD is your shade on your right hand.  
6 The sun shall not strike you by day,  
 nor the moon by night.

And so also in stanza 4, the second verse parallels the first by heightening its thought.

⁷ The LORD will keep you from all evil;  
 he will keep your life.  
⁸ The LORD will keep your going out and your coming in  
 from this time forth and forevermore.

In all these, the second line of each verse parallels the first, and the second verse of each stanza parallels the first also. Commentators often point out a further developed parallelism as well—_anadiplosis_, or step parallelism. In this technique a word in one verse is repeated in the next, leaving the effect of “stepping up.” This may be illustrated with the use of bold text:

1 I lift up my eyes to the hills.  
 From where does **my help** come?  
2 **My help** comes from the LORD,  
 who made heaven and earth.  
3 He will not let your foot be moved;  
 he who **keeps** you will not slumber.  
4 Behold, he who **keeps** Israel  
 will neither slumber nor sleep.  
5 **The LORD** is your keeper;  
 **the LORD** is your shade on your right hand.  
6 The sun shall not strike you by day,  
 nor the moon by night.  
7 **The LORD** will keep you from all evil;  
 he will keep your life.  
8 **The LORD** will keep your going out and your coming in  
 from this time forth and forevermore.¹⁰

The psalmist employs various other poetic devices also. “Heaven and earth” (121:2) is a merism, a figure of speech by which two polar extremes are mentioned to represent the whole: God is the Creator of all things. “By day or by night” (121:6) is a merism for “all the time,” and “your going out and your coming in” (121:8) is a merism for all your activities. “He will not let your foot be moved” (121:3) is a metonymy referring to the stability of the whole of life in God’s care. “Neither slumber nor sleep” is a pleonasm (redundancy), stressing that God’s care is unceasing. Both “your shade” and “at your right hand” (121:5) are metaphors symbolizing God’s protection from harm. Psalm 121:6 is a perfect chiasm in the Hebrew, the two halves of the verse pivoting on the verb “will not smite”: “By day the sun will not smite you, / nor the moon by night” (121:6 NIV). Both “sun” and “moon” are metaphors of various kinds of dangers. “From all evil” (121:7) is another pivot: “The LORD will keep you from all evil; / he will keep your life.” “Your life” here is another metonymy, referring to the person himself. “Your going out and your coming in” (121:8) is a merism descriptive, again, of the whole of life. And throughout the psalm the psalter employs extensive repetition of both verbal terminology and concepts.¹¹

We may outline the thought of this psalm broadly:

121:1–2: Affirmation of trust (stanza 1)  
121:3–8: Assurance of protection (stanzas 2–4)

Throughout this psalm the psalmist affirms the truth of God’s protective care over and over again. He has highlighted various aspects of that protective care, but it is this single theme that he drives repeatedly throughout. Repetition is a common feature of the poetry of the Psalms, but there is a didactic purpose also, as Calvin points out in his closing comments on this psalm:

Such repetition seems at first sight superfluous; but when we consider how difficult it is to correct our distrust, it will be easily perceived that he does not improperly dwell upon the commendation of the divine providence. How few are to be found who yield to God the honor of being a keeper, in order to their being thence assured of their safety; and led to call upon him in the midst of their perils! On the contrary, even when we seem to have largely experienced what this protection of God implies, we yet instantly tremble at the noise of a leaf falling from a tree, as if God had quite forgotten us. Being then entangled in so many unholy misgivings, and so much inclined to distrust, we are taught from the passage that if a sentence couched in a few words does not suffice us, we should gather together whatever may be found throughout the whole Scriptures concerning the providence of God, until this doctrine—that God always keeps watch for us—is deeply rooted in our hearts; so that depending upon his guardianship alone we may bid adieu to all the vain confidences of the world.¹⁵

**Further Reflections**

Psalm 121 is a psalm of trust. It expresses a firm confidence in God’s protective care. On the face of it, the assurance is absolute, unconditional, and for all of life. Many have puzzled over this psalm, wondering how the idea that “no evil will befall you” corresponds with the reality of the suffering of God’s people. Indeed, we might ask if any Israelite who sang this psalm en route to Jerusalem had a misstep along the way (121:3).

But we must recall the royal orientation of the Psalter, and Eaton points us to such a focus here:

Surely the person in question is most likely to be the king, to whom such elaborate pledges are given elsewhere. Yahweh is at his right hand (v. 5). Corresponding to the style of royal avowals of trust (“Yahweh is my —”), the minister assures him “Yahweh is thy keeper, Yahweh is thy shade.” The expression “go out and come in” is often (though not exclusively) used of kings, especially of warfare. The help will be “from now and until eternity.”¹⁶

Bullock notes a striking resemblance of Psalm 121 to Psalm 91, which we will argue is messianic in outlook.¹⁷ If the “I” and “my” of 121:1–2 are the king, then it is his certain success that is assured. We therefore have ground to read the psalm with messianic overtones also and with assurances of his kingdom advance.

And yet by extension we who follow the king and trust in God’s all-encompassing and infallible providence may sing the psalm with him who is the pioneer of our faith (Heb. 12:1). The ancient pilgrim sang this psalm “as he (left) his home to meet the LORD in the Great Congregation at Jerusalem; and the believer (like the Master) sings it as he journeys through earth to the New Jerusalem.”¹⁸ The psalm’s promise for us “does not imply a cushioned life, but a well-armed one”¹⁹ in the knowledge of God’s safe keeping. Its assurance that “no evil” will befall the pilgrim on his journey echoes in our Lord’s affirmation of his protective presence with us always through this life (Matt. 28:20; cf. 6:25–34; Phil. 4:19) and forever (John 10:27–30; 17:10–15). God will keep us from falling and infallibly bring us to our appointed goal (Jude 24–25). In the hands of our Redeemer we are assured that we are safe forever (Rom. 8:28–39).
